# The Coen Brothers' New Netflix Movie Is What TV Wishes It Could Be

The winter-season Coen brothers release is, at this point, tradition, which makes the last five or so years feel bereft in retrospect. The last time Joel and Ethan dropped a movie around this time was 2013, when the gray-tinted, spiritually bleak Inside Llewyn Davis opened in early December; between then and now rests Hail Caesar!, one of their rare screwball comedies, which was given a February ‘16 release likely for either baffling or otherwise aggravating reasons.

Now comes The Ballad of Buster Scruggs, once a Netflix TV series, now a 133-minute omnibus film, a six-chapter saga of the West, liberally seasoned with gallows humor, violence, treachery, a dash of romance, and a bit of melancholy. Maybe you’ve had fortune to watch it on a big screen; now you’ll get to see it again. Maybe you've been waiting for the film to be made available on streaming, in which case you'll be able to watch it for the first time from the comfort of your couch on November 16. However you view it, The Ballad of Buster Scruggs, more than a lyrically titled anthology oddity, begs your consideration.

Maybe the most you know about the film orbits news briefs about the Coens’ efforts to edit it into one movie — which, granted, demands attention in our age of peak television. But there are more reasons to shuffle The Ballad of Buster Scruggs on to your queue than that.

Getting the obvious out of the way: If you’re any kind of movie fan, you at least like the Coens’ work, whether consensus efforts like Fargo or O Brother, Where Art Thou?, quiet masterpieces like A Serious Man, offbeat comedies and cult movies like The Big Lebowski or Burn After Reading or The Hudsucker Proxy, or action masterpieces like Blood Simple or Oscar Best Picture winner No Country for Old Men. There’s a Coen film for everyone.

Buster Scruggs is the best Western of 2018.

A small honor, perhaps, but a significant honor. This year is peppered with Westerns that qualify as some combination of “quirky” or “eccentric” or “innovative,” like Nathan and David Zellner’s Damsel and Jacques Audiard’s The Sisters Brothers. (If you feel like reaching, add Westworld, though take care not to pop a shoulder joint.) The Western has been defined over a century by formula, rewritten and deviated from, including with the marriage of varying subgenres and Western tropes. The Ballad of Buster Scruggs understands the Western’s function in American cinema and its fluidity: Westerns can be musical, they can be humorous, they can be bloody. They’re sweeping and tragic, ghastly and stark. The Ballad of Buster Scruggs is all of these, and without diluting its narrative ambidexterity.

Buster Scruggs is a new kind of Western.

“New” may be a stretch. (Recall the shoulder joint.) The Western blueprint has been examined and remade a hundred times over through cinema history, and masculinity — specifically high ideals of honor, bravery, and stoicism — has been the issue at hand in any number of Western classics, from The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance to Shane. The Ballad of Buster Scruggs can, perhaps should, be enjoyed foremost as a superb example of the Western done well, and done through a handful of different genre mutations, too. But to track the arc of the film from its bonkers opening segment (from which it gets its title) to its somber climax is to see the Western lovingly, judiciously scrutinized by the Coens, and presented afresh.

The Ballad of Buster Scruggs is made handsomely, thanks to costuming choices and set design and most of all through the lens of cinematographer Bruno Delbonnel, lending layers of texture and granular detail to the Coens’ portrait of the American frontier. But these are the traits we expect of Coen movies. What’s especially compelling about The Ballad of Buster Scruggs is how, starting with the Western’s best-recognized elements, verbose confabulations, and the search for justice in an unjust land — and continuing with a complex mix of bittersweet wistfulness, morbid amorality, and existential gloom — the Coens restructure the genre. They’ve made a Western about Westerns, if you’ll excuse the navel-gazing. They want their audience to think about how they think about Westerns by tinkering with its conventions.

Men cutting macho figures, whether James Franco (in the segment “Near Algodones”) or Bill Heck (in “The Gal Who Got Rattled”), show sentiment in either brief flashes or steady streams. Nonthreatening aw-shucks types, like Tim Blake Nelson (in the film’s namesake segment, “The Ballad of Buster Scruggs”), are preternaturally gifted in the art of killing and unafraid to use their gifts. Legendary grizzled tough guy Liam Neeson (“Meal Ticket”) is instead a pathetic, vulturous old drunk. The nattily attired duo (Brendan Gleeson and Jojo O’Neill) sharing a carriage with perfect strangers in “The Mortal Remains” are bounty hunters of a sort, together casting an eerie and otherworldly shadow.

Nothing’s what it seems in The Ballad of Buster of Scruggs. That’s the purpose of the exercise. The Coens embrace all the delights the Western has to offer, but in keeping with their preference for feel-bad vibes and in marrying that with intermittent but genuine tenderness, their gifts seep into stories equally strange and sad.

Tim Blake Nelson and Tom Waits

There’s not a weak spot in The Ballad of Buster Scruggs’ ensemble, but there are standouts, and of those, Nelson and Waits are MVPs. Watching Nelson do his thing in “The Ballad of Buster Scruggs,” you might wonder if he found inspiration for the part in either Evil Stewie or Mr. Happy Face; he’s a psychopathic murderer who kills with a smile on his face and a song in his heart, as quick to gun you down as sing improvised elegies in your name the moment your corpse hits the ground. Nelson’s the benign sort, not the kind of actor typically associated with characters like Scruggs, which makes him perfectly suited to play this charming, unassumingly lethal lunatic.

Then there’s Waits, already a legendary musician and having a great year on the big screen in both this and The Old Man & the Gun, in which he’s more or less cast as himself: an eccentric storyteller who spins tales in pleasingly roundabout ways. Here, he plays a prospector relieving the land of its gold bounty in “All Gold Canyon,” setting down camp on green pastures nestled against a river bend. It’s a Western, so eventually he’s done wrong by a side-winding rogue without honor but, in the end, he triumphs, his guts intact. The segment reads like a Waits song distilled into cinema, the kind of tune he’d write in tribute to the prospector himself.

Everyone will walk away from The Ballad of Buster Scruggs with their favorite bits, but these two are easily the most complete and cast with the most fascinating leads.

Movies > TV.

Television can be a drag. The time investment it requires ranges from sizable to immense, and there’s so much TV that even devoting yourself to a handful of series is overwhelming, especially when one goes off the rails and you consider everything else you're missing.

But movies end! In relatively brief amounts of time! That the Coens repurposed The Ballad of Buster Scruggs as a feature is to its benefit. It’s hard to picture what “The Ballad of Buster Scruggs” segment might look like, how it might play, at 45 minutes to an hour. (Editor's note: In a recent interview with The Playlist, Zoe Kazan refuted prior reporting that The Ballad of Buster Scruggs was originally conceived as a television series, stating, "We thought we were making exactly what is coming out now.") As is, each part is given room to breathe based on what it’s about. The meatier the material, the more time afforded. Simple. And the structure works, too, never demanding that you give it a chance to improve or asking you for more time to find its rhythm. It establishes its rhythm from the very start, weird as it is wild, grisly, brutal, and abidingly maudlin.

The Ballad of Buster Scruggs is what a movie should be — and what television wishes it could be.
