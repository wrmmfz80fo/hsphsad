# 'True Detective' Proves Mahershala Ali Might Be the Greatest Actor of His Generation

[Mahershala Ali Might Be](https://copperglow.dns0663.com/blog/7705255177.html)

When Mahershala Ali walked up to accept his Best Supporting Actor Oscar for Moonlight in 2017, hardly anyone knew who he was.

That changed almost immediately after his speech. “It’s not about you. It’s about these characters,” he said, describing his job while tearing up, yet still maintaining the gravitas that makes it impossible to look away from his performances. Anyone who saw Moonlight concurred that Ali was a highlight in a small role as a father figure to a boy (and later a man) struggling to come to terms with his sexuality amid the backdrop of a broken home in Miami.

Though he’s now 44, and spent the bulk of his career in TV roles, Ali is not wasting any time on his way to deserved prominence. He followed up the Oscar with a win for his supporting role in last year’s Green Book at the recent Golden Globes, where Bill Murray happened to spill vodka into his eye (he forgave the comedy legend, though). Even more interesting than his captivating work in that movie as a well-educated jazz pianist who decides to rove through the Deep South on tour with Viggo Mortensen’s brassy Italian driver/bodyguard, however, is what he does as the sole lead in the new and much more taut third season of HBO’s True Detective (premiering January 13).

True Detective has had a troubled road, to say the least. The first season was magnetic, in no small part due to the chemistry and imitable talent of Matthew McConaughey and Woody Harrelson as buddy cops and sometimes enemies. The second season lost its way when creator and writer Nic Pizzolatto’s ambitions got wider than what he could credibly deliver, resulting in terrible reviews and a fan backlash.

The latest season returns to the sort of scope we saw the first time, following Ali’s Wayne Hays, a state detective in Arkansas trying to piece together the mystery behind the disappearance of two young kids shortly after Halloween in 1980. As in that gem of a first season, this new one shifts through time periods, centering on Hays’ original detective work as well as his recollections and attempts to find closure years later.

It’s rife with enticing mystery, but also much more. What makes this new True Detective ultimately work, at least based on the handful of episodes offered to press, is that it strips its story down to an elemental backwoods detective yarn that chases leads and presents potential red herrings (Mamie Gummer, daughter of Meryl Streep, is unshakeable as the hollering and confused mother of the missing kids).

But it's Ali's carefully unfurled narrative that deepens the season beyond what any casual viewer would expect. His Hays starts as a serious but jaded detective working alongside Stephen Dorff’s delightfully silly, white partner Roland West, whose career advances thanks largely to the color of his skin. Hays' world is radically transformed by not only the children’s mysterious disappearance, but also his sudden feelings of love for a teacher who’s connected to those kids (a fantastic Carmen Ejogo).

Then things get much, much more interesting. We watch Hays struggle over the unsolved case in 1990 and 2015 (in the latter year he’s being interviewed for a true-crime series). But he isn’t struggling in the usual grumbling-detective way. Instead, we find out that in 2015, he’s coping with an earlier stage of dementia (hinted at from the first episode with a reference to his memory problems), which threatens to shatter the memories that are critical to him finally finding resolution in the case that has dogged him.

To watch Ali through all three periods of this show is to watch an actor at the height of his powers, controlling each iteration of Hays in sublime ways. (He clearly wasn't joking about giving his whole self over to his characters.) The actor gives us insight we would’ve never expected into Hays, who’s great at his job but not always at relating to others. Then he delivers another Hays, this one in a happy marriage that threatens to turn sour because of his preoccupations. And then, most masterfully of all, another Hays who's retired and forced to reckon with what he put behind him and never paid attention to, including in his love life, as his own grip of history is rapidly fading from his mind.

Even with less dialogue than the supremely talk-y first True Detective season, the entire drama is written on Ali’s face. He’s somehow able to communicate the elation and devastation of discovery in a younger detective as well as the more sobering moment when he realizes years later that it wasn’t as it all seemed. It’s not a mere cable-TV turn: It’s an announcement that Ali, just after we learned his name, needs to be considered in any conversation about the best male actors of his generation.
