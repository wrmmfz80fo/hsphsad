# You: Stronger, Faster, Smarter

It's a strange feeling to be alone in a room with a monkey. I think it's because we expect monkeys to act like dogs. But then they do these incredibly human things: yawning, stretching, scratching their ears. Or they stare at you, as if they're sizing you up. It's hard not to feel like you're visiting a distant cousin or a smaller, more basic, more lovable version of yourself. Evolution is easy to accept when it's just you and a monkey.

I come to understand this while standing inside the Andersen Neural Prosthetics laboratory at Caltech University in Pasadena, California. With me is a rhesus macaque monkey named Chewie. Chewie is sitting in a chair, his neck in a harness. A pink acrylic plate is attached to his head, and a hole in the plate leads to an opening in his skull. Three thin electrodes are threaded into this hole, touching Chewie's brain; they measure his brain activity. In front of him is an infrared camera -- to track his eye movement -- and a computer screen.

Chewie doesn't seem to mind this arrangement. But then, how would I know? As 31-year-old Grant Mulliken, the neuroscientist who handles Chewie, points out, "It's hard to be a monkey psychologist."

Like everyone at the lab, Mulliken is exceptionally intelligent, well trained, and well intentioned. He's also well versed in everything from computer science and statistics to neuroscience and sociobiology. He assures me that every precaution is taken to ensure the health and well-being of Chewie and his fellow monkeys: veterinarians on staff, treats from Trader Joe's, social time with other monkeys, an early retirement to a monkey country club. So I don't feel bad for Chewie. In fact, I'm a little in awe.

Chewie, you see, is telepathic. He's able to communicate with a computer using only his brain. All he has to do is think about moving a small white dot on a computer monitor, and -- presto! -- the cursor goes there. Then he gets some Tang. I can't even get my dog to roll over.

Chewie doesn't know it, but his work will eventually allow humans to be fitted with wireless robotic hands, arms, and legs -- to become, in essence, bionic. (Recently, a 26-year-old paralyzed Massachusetts man became the first person to replicate Chewie's cursor-moving experiment.) Ultimately, this research will give humans the ability to control anything with their thoughts, and machines will become extensions of the mind.

INSTANT MESSENGERS

It all starts with neurons, the electrochemical messengers in the brain that tell the body what to do. The human brain has about 100 billion. One group of neurons allows you to flex your toes, others work together to enable speech, and still others may make you simultaneously nostalgic, amorous, and aware of the heartbreaking brevity of your own life as you walk across a field of fresh-cut grass on a warm spring day.

A monkey's brain works similarly. When Chewie reaches for something, various regions of his brain become active, including the primary motor cortex (which tells his muscles what to do) and the posterior parietal cortex (which tells his hand what the end goal of his reach is). It is these regions of the brain that most interest scientists.

By monitoring Chewie's brain while the monkey uses a joystick to move a cursor across a computer screen, Mulliken can determine which neurons are firing and how fast. Certain neurons become active when the monkey moves the joystick left, and a different group fires when the monkey reaches right, or up or down, and so on. Thus, each movement of Chewie's hand has a specific electrical signature, and researchers at the Andersen lab have developed a computer program that recognizes these signatures and responds accordingly. Chewie, who is, after all, just a lazy primate, quickly figures out that he gets his Tang even if he doesn't physically move the joystick. He gets it for merely thinking about where he wants the cursor to go. So he stops moving the joystick, but the cursor moves anyway. Bingo -- a telepathic monkey.

It's not difficult to imagine the potential human applications. Electrodes could connect to a small relay device that sends the brain waves to a decoding computer, which figures out where the brain is telling an arm or leg to move. That external device then sends a signal to a robotic arm or leg, which responds accordingly.

There are, of course, enormous challenges to overcome. First, researchers have to figure out what all the different neural signals mean. Scientists already have a rough idea, at least in two dimensions (on a computer screen), at least with monkeys. The ability to work three-dimensionally is not a huge leap, but it does require an extraordinary amount of trial and error. Imagine a jigsaw puzzle with a few billion pieces.

The second major obstacle is that the brain attacks foreign matter in its midst via a process called gliosis. As glial cells swarm an electrode in self-defense, they choke off its signal. To address this problem, the lab is working on an electrode array that periodically repositions itself near a new batch of neurons. Since the electrodes are smaller than a human hair, damage to the brain is nil.

The third and biggest challenge has to do with heat. All telemetric (wireless) devices create heat. This means that, as of now, the electrode wires must go from inside the brain, through the skull, to a device bolted to the outside of the head, where a battery would be. This is both unsightly and prone to infection, and researchers have not yet settled upon a solution. "We'll solve this problem before we get into the clinical phase, which is about 2 to 3 years away," says neuroscientist Dan Rizzuto, Ph.D., Mulliken's colleague.

In addition to neurally controlled limbs, research is proceeding on other neural prosthetic devices: brain implants for Parkinson's patients; retinal and occipital-lobe implants that promise sight to the blind; and something called a bion, an implantable nerve stimulator the size of a pen cap that may someday cure everything from epilepsy to erectile dysfunction.

The field has exploded in the past 5 years. Which raises the question, Why now? The answer: computing power. Tracking and processing all this information in real time requires incredibly fast processors.

The irony, of course, is that the more scientists learn about the brain, the more

it becomes clear how feeble computing power is in comparison to the brain itself. The obvious thing to do, then, has been to reverse-engineer the brain--figure out how it works and copy it. "The brain is, in some sense, a computer," Mulliken says. "There are a lot of reasons to try to understand how it processes information within its network of neurons. One is for the heck of it, the fun of it. But the brain is also very efficient at doing a lot of things that we can't do very well." We are chatting outside Chewie's room as he says this, standing in front of a massive console of keyboards and screens, all working in concert to read the monkey's mind. "But the brain is so baffling, you realize that it's a much more complicated machine than anything we've ever built.

"Vision is a perfect example," he says, all geeky and inspired. "The problems that have been solved by our visual system are amazing. Computer vision can't touch it." For this reason, perhaps the greatest leap forward in the field of neural prosthetics has come in the area of vision, with a program called Second Sight.

Meet Harold Churchey. He's a 78-year-old man from Maryland who looks a bit like Mr. Magoo. Churchey has retinitis pigmentosa, a congenital condition that causes the thin layer of tissue in the back of the eye to deteriorate. He had limited vision in his left eye most of his life and lost it completely in 1985; his right eye has always been sightless. I meet him one evening in a hotel lobby in Burbank. I'd heard that he is a pioneer of sorts.

Seventeen years ago, an ophthalmologist from Duke University named Mark Humayun, armed with powerful computers and microsurgical tools, set out to discover how to restore sight to the blind. "The big breakthrough," he told me at his office at the USC medical campus, "was when we put an electrode across a patient's eye wall onto the retina. For about 15 to 20 minutes, he didn't see anything. It was pin-drop silence. I mean, we were checking every possible circuit. Then the guy said, 'Oh, are you talking about that little tiny light that keeps flickering?' That was one of the most exciting moments of my life."

That patient was Harold Churchey. "That was the first time I ever saw anything in my right eye," says Churchey. "Just that little yellow light. It looked like the sun."

USING THE EYES AS A WINDOW

The eye is not merely an external camera that sends information to the occipital lobe -- it processes the information first. The roughly 100 million photoreceptors (rods and cones) in the eye capture light, allowing the retina to process the information and send an electrical impulse to the brain over 1.2 million nerve fibers. Dr. Humayun, who also has a Ph.D. in biomedical engineering, discovered how to surgically place a tiny electrode on top of those neurons to stimulate them. When the electrodes fired an electrical current, Churchey saw light.

Dr. Humayun's next challenge was to make this process work with a camera. His team spent years developing an algorithm whereby a camera could send impulses to electrodes on the retina that would make sense to the neurons in the eye. The camera's computer and the neurons had to speak the same language. This was, of course, difficult -- like trying to decode UNIX when all you know is cuneiform. Churchey was the subject of much of this research, patiently returning to the lab for surgeries every few years as Dr. Humayun tested new algorithms and devices.

Finally, in February 2002, Dr. Humayun told Churchey that a working prototype was ready. The device, which was mounted on Churchey's right eye, was a tiny camera connected to a pair of glasses; an array of 16 electrodes sprouted from the frames and were hardwired into Churchey's eye.

Churchey explains what happened next: "They hooked me up and put a big L on the wall. I didn't know what it was at first. I had to scan it, to look for edges. Backward. Forward. I had a time doing it, but I finally found it." Churchey's "sight" wasn't vision in the conventional sense. What Dr. Humayun had given him was something akin to a football scoreboard of 16 flickering lights.

Dr. Humayun is currently working on a device that uses 60 electrodes implanted on the retinal wall, rather than 16. One with 1,000 will follow that. As you might imagine, image resolution is a function of the number of electrodes -- at least that's Dr. Humayun's guess. No one knows for sure. "It's nerve-racking," he says. "It's not that the nerve cells aren't there. The question is, Are we smart enough to figure out how to connect them to the brain?"

Suddenly, all this seems pretty sci-fi to me. Advances in neural prosthetics require knowledge, in some form or another, of everything from quantum mechanics and microbiology to software engineering, surgical techniques, and the theory of evolution. Consider how many accumulated years of knowledge we're talking about: millions of people discussing, questioning, researching, studying, writing, so that now, the beginning of the 21st century, we're finally able to begin to speak to the brain itself in its own language.

And the thing is, it's all still so primitive, so limited. I ask Churchey how he feels about being the first human to be given artificial sight. He laughs. "I'm just an old blind man," he says. He pauses, then adds, "The good Lord has taken care of me for 78 years. I have no fear."

Churchey stops, then turns to me. "Do you believe in God's word?" I answer uncomfortably, because I'm not, you know, a church-goer, and that's always awkward -- especially if you're talking to an endearing old blind man who is now able to see. I wanted to tell him to believe in science instead, since that's what gave him sight. But how do you say that?

"Well, you look at Daniel 12:4," he goes on. "God says what's technology today is obsolete tomorrow, in any field." It amazes me how much this resembles Moore's law, the famous notion that the number of transistors that can fit on a computer chip doubles every 18 months, and thus overall computing power increases exponentially. It's held roughly true for 40 years.

What does it mean that we'll soon be able to change the very nature of what we are? Eventually, someone will give this technology to a Major League pitcher, a football player, a baby, a soldier, and they'll be able to do things that human beings have never done before. They'll be stronger, faster, smarter. Humans, version 2.0.

There's a word for this -- a term that describes the ability to adapt to the environment, to change from something less capable to something more capable. The word is "evolution." And the question is, Are we beginning to engineer our own evolution?

Man's adaptation to nature has always been driven by the complexity, flexibility, and raw creative power of his brain--specifically, the ability of that brain to create and modify tools. The thing is, for the past 200 years, we've been creating tools that help us make more tools. That's all a computer is. A simple device that works a little like the brain and is good at rote tasks. It's not as if we'll ever train a computer to do something our brains can't.

The only step that neural prosthetics takes is allowing the computer to do this work for the brain while inside the body, while linked up directly to the brain. It isn't really that much of a leap. And though it's cool to think of Chewie and his peers -- our evolutionary precursors -- essentially teaching us to create our evolutionary heirs, the idea of putting a computer in the brain is only natural. It's nature's closest equivalent anyway.
